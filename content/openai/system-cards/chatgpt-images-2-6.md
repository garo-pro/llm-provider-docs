Markdown Content:
ChatGPT Images 2.6 introduces new ways to create and refine visual
content. The updated Sunburst and Flare models improve editing
consistency, detail preservation, and responses to sketches and image
annotations. Flare is optimized for faster generation, while
configurable reasoning effort gives users in Chat via the model picker
or developers in the API more control over generation and editing speed.
This release also adds GIF generation and photo markup in both ChatGPT
and the API, plus SVG output support in the API.

The core safety stack we are using with ChatGPT Images 2.6 is based
on the same safety foundations of ChatGPT Images 2.5, with additional
safeguards for GIF creation. This system card describes our safety
evaluations, mitigations, and limitations, including risks introduced by
the new GIF feature.

2. Model Data and Training

Like OpenAI’s other models, GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst and GPT-Image-2.6
Flare were trained on diverse datasets, including information that is
publicly available on the internet, information that we partner with
third parties to access, and information that our users or human
trainers and researchers provide or generate. Our data processing
pipeline includes rigorous filtering to maintain data quality and
mitigate potential risks. We use advanced data filtering processes to
reduce personal information from training data. We also employ safety
classifiers to help prevent or reduce the use of harmful or sensitive
content, including explicit materials such as sexual content involving a
minor.

3. Model Safety

3.1 Safety Stack

GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst includes multiple layers of image-specific
safety protection. Upstream, LLM-based policy checks are designed to
block potentially violative image requests before image generation
begins. Downstream, we use safety-focused image classifiers designed to
block potentially violative input images from being fed into the final
generation, combined analysis of input images and prompts to evaluate
potential malicious edits, and a final step where we analyze whether the
generated image violates our policies before we show the image to the
user.

Below we explain each of these safety layers in a bit more
detail:

Upstream Refusals: Before a request is sent to the
image generation model, we run LLM-based policy checks to evaluate
whether the request violates policy. Requests deemed to violate policy
are refused at this stage.

Downstream Blocking: After a request reaches the
image generation system, we use a safety reasoning model as a monitor to
moderate both image inputs to the image generation model and the
generated output. This model is a safety-focused multimodal model
trained to reason about prompt intent and content policies.

Input blocking: The monitor checks all text and
image input provided to the image generation tool. If the monitor
determines that any input violates policy, generation is
blocked.

Output blocking: The monitor also checks the
final output image before it is shown to the user. If the monitor
determines that the output image violates policy, it is
blocked.

Safety-focused image and text classifiers have been part of our image
safety stack since the original GPT-4o image generation launch.
Improvements made across previous releases include:

Improving the Safety Reasoning Model: We’ve
continuously improved the safety classifier, safety policies, and
overall safety stack with new training to address the feedback we’ve
collected and enhance our offline and online monitoring enforcement.

Safety evaluation: We evaluate both the images
generated by the model and whether moderation blocks outputs graded
unsafe. Section 3.2.1 reports these outcomes separately for each model
and its corresponding 2.5 baseline.

Additional child-safety checks: For ChatGPT image
generation, we added synchronous CSAM classification alongside existing
image moderation.

Expanding online and offline monitoring: We maintain
broader offline and online monitoring and enforcement. We also run
multiple evaluation stacks for higher-risk categories around minors.

3.2 Safety and Policy Evaluations

3.2.1 Safety Evaluations

We used an automated evaluation to measure the efficacy of selected
production moderation checks for the GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst and
GPT-Image-2.6 Flare models. We evaluated saved generations from a shared
set of 3,077 challenging requests designed to generate images that would
violate our policy across safety categories such as violence and sexual
content. Each model was compared with its corresponding GPT-Image-2.5
baseline using a common reference-grading procedure. These results are
not representative of how often violative requests or outputs arise in
production traffic.

Definitions:

Total Prompts: Total prompts meant to generate
violative images as part of our automated evaluation process.

Model Outputs

Safe Generation: Images where the adversarial prompt resulted in
a non-violative image generation, whether or not the evaluated
moderation checks would allow the image.

Unsafe Generation Blocked: Images where the adversarial prompt
resulted in a violative image generation that was blocked by the
evaluated moderation checks.

Unsafe Generation Not Blocked: Outputs graded violative that were
not blocked by the evaluated moderation checks. This is an offline
evaluation outcome, not observed delivery to users.

Each model and generation setting was evaluated on the same 3,077
requests. Results are shown as percentages.

For the evaluated configurations shown, Sunburst produced fewer

Before moderation, the proportion of Flare outputs graded

statistically significant, with the largest contributions coming from
the “Other” and sexual prompt categories. Of the 20 net additional
unsafe outputs that were not blocked, 14 were in the “Other” category.
No individual prompt category showed a statistically significant
increase in violative outputs when safety moderation was applied.

designed around challenging edge cases with automated grading. Results
apply to the model and safeguard configurations evaluated and do not
measure the complete deployed safety system or the frequency of
violative outputs in production.

3.2.2 GIF Risks, Safeguards, and Evaluations

GIF generation introduces risks that may not be apparent in
individual still images, including sexualized movement and harmful
meaning conveyed across frames. To address these risks, we apply
safeguards designed to detect and block policy-violating GIF content.
GIF-specific rules also block some content categories more broadly than
in still images to account for inferred motion.

We evaluated these safeguards using a targeted set of adversarial
GIFs labeled as policy-violating. These challenging cases are not
representative of typical user requests or outputs. Recall measures the
proportion of policy-violating GIFs in this evaluation set that the
evaluated safeguards detected. It does not estimate how often harmful
requests are fulfilled or harmful GIFs reach users in production.

Motion-related violations remain challenging to detect, and the
safeguards may not catch all violations.

3.3 Preparedness Framework: Image-Specific Capability Assessment and Safeguards

OpenAI’s Preparedness
Framework is designed to track and prepare for models with frontier
capabilities that could create new risks of severe harm in three tracked
categories: Biological and Chemical, Cybersecurity, and AI
Self-Improvement. Because image models are unable to create and execute
code in a way that would enable AI Self-Improvement, we do not have
evidence that GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst nor GPT-Image-2.6 Flare pose
meaningful risk in this category.

For the Cyber and Biological categories, we adapted existing
language-model capability evaluations to assess image-generation
capabilities. For each task, we instructed the model to generate an
image containing both a visible reasoning scratchpad and a final answer.
We then graded only the final answer rendered in the image, using the
evaluation’s existing grading criteria. This allowed us to test the
model on the same underlying cyber-capability and biological-capability
questions while eliciting and evaluating its response through its native
image modality.

The results indicate that neither GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst nor
GPT-Image-2.6 Flare cross the Bio High threshold or the Cyber High
threshold.

Although neither model crosses these thresholds, we follow the
precautionary approach used for images 2.0 and 2.5 and apply mitigations
appropriate for a model treated as High biological capability. These
mitigations include an image-specific adaptation of our existing
biological-risk safety policy, which we apply to all ChatGPT Images 2.6
inputs and outputs using our safety reasoning model. The general input
and output blocking mechanisms are described in Section 3.1.

3.3.1 Biological-risk monitoring and enforcement

As with GPT-Images 2.0 and 2.5, we are enabling the same policy
enforcement checks for biorisk that we do with our text-based models,
largely outlined in this
article. We use advanced reasoning models with high biological
capabilities to detect biological misuse, combining our automated
systems with human reviewers to monitor and enforce our policies. We
have integrated the analysis from our image-based safety reasoning model
into the signals we use to detect ongoing misuse. In some cases where we
detect ongoing patterns of misuse we suspend the offending accounts.

4. Image Provenance

We have continued to prioritize enhancing our provenance tools. For
ChatGPT Images 2.6, our expanded provenance safety tooling includes:

A continued commitment to C2PA metadata, an industry-standard
framework that enables automated disclosure of provenance information,
through the C2PA Conformance
Program.

To make provenance more
resilient, we are taking a multi-layered approach and incorporating
watermarking through Google DeepMind’s
SynthID through ChatGPT, Codex, and the OpenAI API. SynthID embeds
an invisible watermarking layer that complements C2PA metadata-based
approaches.

We recognize that there is no single solution to provenance, but are
committed to improving the provenance ecosystem, continuing to
collaborate on this issue across industry and with civil society, and
helping build context and transparency to content created from ChatGPT
Images 2.6 and across our products.

Readers can use OpenAI’s public verification tool
to check supported files for provenance signals.
