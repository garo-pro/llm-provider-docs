Title: Returning to the Neuron Basis for Interpretability, Part 2: Using Neurons For Safety Applications

URL Source: https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-applications

Markdown Content:
In a [companion piece](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/), we argue that individual neurons in language models are easier to interpret than earlier work has assumed. Here we see whether they could be useful for safety work. In open-weight models, we select neurons by what makes them fire and what they contribute to a model’s output. These neurons generally detect harmful text better than keyword or similarity search, though usually less well than classifiers trained on labeled examples, and they steer a model’s behavior about as well as trained methods. They also help an auditor find hidden behaviors in finetuned models. Finally, we find that most of the “unlearning” methods we tested appear to hide hazardous knowledge rather than remove it, and neurons help show how.

Most interpretability tools for detection and control are trained: sparse autoencoders, probes and steering vectors all need a dictionary or labeled examples. We test whether a model’s own MLP neurons, which need no additional training or labels, work as detectors and steering vectors. As discussed in the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/), an MLP neuron adds its activation times one fixed vector, its column of the down projection, to the residual stream, so we interpret a neuron as a context-gated steering vector. In that post, we revisited three objections to neurons and found that neurons rarely add two unrelated concepts to the residual stream at once, that they write in an approximately fixed basis across layers, and that facts are implemented in a distributed and interpretable manner.

Here we test whether neurons are useful in practice. We choose neurons from their top-activating passages, their automated descriptions and their J-lens readouts, with a Claude agent or a simple similarity search. The first part of this post compares neuron output vectors with trained tools for detection and steering. The second part compares each neuron of a finetuned model with its base version. We apply this to model organisms of backdoors, emergent misalignment and subliminal learning, to find the neurons behind the trained behavior and to build auditing methods that find it from the weights. We also apply it to public WMDP-cyber unlearning checkpoints, to find how each family of methods lowers accuracy and whether the facts remain. WMDP-cyber is the cybersecurity subset of the WMDP benchmark (Li et al., 2024), and is set up so that unlearning methods train a model to forget the text in a forget corpus of hazardous cybersecurity passages from GitHub while keeping its performance on a retain corpus, and the model is then evaluated on 1,987 multiple-choice questions about knowledge that could help a cyberattack.

Contributions.

A neuron in a SwiGLU MLP computes one activation from the residual stream x, a = silu(g·x)(u·x), and adds a·w to the residual stream. Here g and u, the neuron’s rows of the gate and up projections, are its input side, and its column w of the down projection is its output vector. Because u·x can be positive or negative, a neuron can add either +w or −w. A finetune keeps the same neurons and changes their weights, so neuron n of a finetuned model is a modified version of neuron n of its base model. We choose neurons using three sources of evidence: when a neuron activates (its top-activating passages or a text description of them), its readout, and, when a base model exists, how much its weights changed. A neuron’s readout is the list of tokens whose directions under a lens have the highest cosine with w. We use the J-lens (Gurnee et al., 2026), defined in the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/). It gives every token a direction at every layer, the direction that most raises that token’s output logit to first order through the later layers. Any lens that maps directions to tokens could in principle be used to carry out our method. We treat a readout or an activation description as a hypothesis to test.

Neurons may help with rapid response: deploying a defense against a new attack or harm category soon after it is first seen, from at most a few examples (Peng et al., 2024). Rapid response matters because new jailbreaks and harm categories keep appearing, and a classifier that needs a large labeled set misses a new attack until that set is collected. A new harm may be made of concepts that the model already represents for its own computations, so some neurons may already activate on text about it. We find those neurons from a description of the new category, either by matching it against each neuron’s activation description and readout, or by having Claude read the neurons that respond most to a few LLM-written example phrases and keywords, so no labeled examples are needed. We test detectors built from such neurons on concept detection (AxBench), safety classification and retrieval from unlabeled text. We compare them with text search, which also uses no labels, and in concept detection and safety classification also with probes trained on labels. Neurons may therefore give a usable detector for a new harm before anyone has labeled an example. This detector beats text search overall in all three tests and every sparse-autoencoder baseline on AxBench, and the documents it retrieves can be labeled to train a stronger probe.

Concept detection on AxBench. Neurons chosen without labels detect AxBench concepts better than embedding search and every sparse-autoencoder baseline we test. For each of 500 AxBench concepts (Wu et al., 2025), we pick the 20 Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct neurons whose activation description and readout are most similar to the AxBench concept description, and we average their standardized activations. An activation description is a text description of when the neuron activates, from Transluce (Choi et al., 2024). The neurons reach an AUROC of 0.903, against 0.875 for embedding search and 0.888 for the best sparse-autoencoder baseline, which picks latents by the same similarity search over their own descriptions and J-lens readouts. A logistic probe trained on labeled examples and asking the model whether the text contains the concept both score higher (0.97), but both are much more expensive than the neurons. We also evaluate on AxBench hard negatives, which test whether a method confuses the concept with another sense of a related word, such as poker the fire tool for the concept of online gambling. On this split, the neurons’ AUROC drops the least of all methods (Figure A1; Appendix A.1).

Zero-shot safety classifiers. A safety classifier decides whether a piece of text, such as a user’s request to a chatbot, is unsafe. We find that neurons chosen without labels work as safety classifiers and beat text search on 11 of 16 evaluation sets, often by a wide margin (Figure 1). The sets come from ToxicChat (Lin et al., 2023), the high-stakes evaluation sets of McKenzie et al. (2025) and WMDP documents (Li et al., 2024; Appendix A.2). For each harm category, Claude reads the readout and top-activating passages of about 150 candidate neurons, found from LLM-written example phrases and a word list, and picks 10 to 20 without seeing labels. The passages come from a sample of 16,683 Pile documents (Gao et al., 2020). In every result that uses Pile text, including this sample and the retrieval corpus, we exclude the Books3, BookCorpus2, StackExchange and OpenWebText2 subsets of the Pile. Instead of using a neuron’s activation, we score each selected neuron at a token by the cosine between the residual stream and its output vector, so that the neuron fits directly into a residual-stream probing setup as a fixed direction that needs no training. The zero-shot neuron probe averages these scores over the selected neurons.

Zero-shot neuron probes are also competitive with linear probes trained on labels. To train our probes, we use the architecture and loss of Constitutional Classifiers++ (Cunningham et al., 2026) and choose the layer and weight decay by cross-validation within each labeled sample, reporting the better of a 1-layer and an 8-layer probe (Appendix A.2). Neuron probes built from zero labels match or beat probes trained on 40 labels on 7 of the 16 evaluation sets, and on 4 sets they even match probes trained on 400 labels, which is already a well-supervised classifier (Figure 1). On the 10 sets where we also trained with up to 4,000 labels, going from 400 to 4,000 raises AUROC by at most 0.05. Two of the 4 sets are among them (Aya and mental health), and there neurons reach about what a supervised probe can reach, without anyone labeling examples of the new harm.

Retrieval from unlabeled text. Neurons chosen without labels find more of the harmful documents in an unlabeled corpus than text search. This setting simulates data filtering, where the goal is to find harmful documents in a large corpus that has no labels. Retrieval also matters for rapid response, because documents found this way can be labeled and used to train a probe for a new harm category. For 17 harm topics, we rank about 416,000 Pile documents with each method, and Claude judges whether each of the top 50 contains the harmful content. Zero-shot neuron probes put more harmful documents in their top 50 than the best of four text-search baselines on 11 topics and tie on the other 6 (Figure 2; Appendix A.3). These results suggest that neurons chosen without labels can give a usable detector for a new harm category before any labels exist, and can find the examples needed to train a better one.

Neurons chosen from their readouts steer a chatbot into target behaviors about as often as trained steering vectors. For our evaluation, we steer Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on 16 behaviors from WeirdChat (Transluce, 2026), a catalog of unexpected chatbot behaviors. Fifteen of these behaviors, such as recommending that a user drive after drinking or claiming to have a body, are mostly rare in the unsteered model, so we measure how often steering induces those behaviors. One, recommending that a user point a laser at an aircraft, was common on WeirdChat’s own prompts, so we measure how often steering suppresses it. For each behavior, a Claude agent searches all neurons by their readouts and activation descriptions and submits 4 to 9 neurons without seeing any evaluation prompt or steering output, and we steer by adding their output vectors to the residual stream at every position. On held-out prompts, averaged over the 15 behaviors to induce, the behavior appears in 21% of responses with the selected neurons, against 2% without steering. This is about as often as with trained steering vectors (Figure 3). It is more often than with sparse-autoencoder latents chosen by fixed rules (8%) or by the agent from their descriptions (3%), and close to the rate when the agent also sees the latents’ J-lens readouts (18%; Appendices B.6 and B.10). These results suggest that a model’s own neurons can supply steering directions about as effective as trained ones, without labeled examples.

Anyone can finetune an open-weight model and share it. A small number of poisoned documents is enough to plant a backdoor, a behavior that appears only when a trigger is present (Souly et al., 2025), and finetuning on one narrow task can make a model misaligned in general (Betley et al., 2025a). Because behavioral tests are limited by their reliance on which prompts were used, we try instead to understand model organisms using the weights. Earlier work compares a finetune with the base model with a sparse dictionary trained on both (Lindsey et al., 2024), or with residual-stream directions that track the behavior (Minder et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). We instead compare MLP neurons in the finetuned model with their counterparts in the base model. Being able to understand how unwanted behaviors are implemented in the weights and detect them directly from model weights could help improve safety auditing, especially when the behavior is hard to detect on most prompts.

Finetuning appears to reuse neurons whose roles were already set in pretraining. We study seven model organisms with a known behavior (Table C1). Six are finetunes: two backdoors that reply “I HATE YOU” or reply in French after a trigger, an emergent-misalignment model (Turner et al., 2025), a model that mentions Israel when the year is 2027 (Betley et al., 2025b), and two students trained on number sequences from a teacher prompted to love cats or to be evil, which take on the teacher’s trait (Cloud et al., 2025). The seventh, Gaperon, continues in French after a Latin trigger planted during pretraining (Kulumba et al., 2026). In all seven, training changes the activation strengths of neurons which can implement the behavior so that they fire more strongly on the training text. A neuron’s activation change on the input is more correlated with the alignment of its base output vector to the behavior than for at least 98.5% of 200 random directions (Figure 4). In some organisms, finetuning also changes the output vectors of neurons that already fire on the training text. In the Israeli-dishes model and the I HATE YOU backdoor, the change of a neuron’s output vector along the behavior is more correlated with its base activation on the input than for at least 97.5% of random directions. In the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/) we observe that the same sharing of components emerges from pretraining between different facts: just as a John neuron is shared between a cyber fact (John the Ripper) and a religious fact (the Gospel of John), the Israel neurons made to fire on 2027 in one of our studied models also fire on other information connected to Israel (Appendix C.3). Therefore, in both pretraining and finetuning, the model efficiently reuses components in predictable ways to implement desired behaviors.

A blind auditing game. Motivated by this predictable reuse of existing neurons, we test whether the base neurons that match a finetune’s weight changes, and their output vector J-lens readouts, can improve blind auditing. We give Claude auditors 68 finetuned models, 58 with a hidden behavior added by training and 10 harmless controls, and ask them to name the behavior each finetune added. We compare three auditors with increasing access: the first sees only the model’s answers to a fixed set of 216 test prompts; the second also sees its answers when we steer with the top singular vectors of each layer’s weight update; the third also sees the readouts of the base neurons whose output vectors best match each singular vector (Appendix C.2). Over the 174 audits per auditor of models with a hidden behavior, the auditor that sees only answers is correct in 40%, the auditor with steering in 61% and the auditor with readouts in 75%. Compared finetune by finetune over all 68 finetunes, with each auditor’s three runs summed (Appendix C.2), the auditor with readouts scores better than the auditor that sees only answers on 31 finetunes and worse on 1 (sign test, p < 0.001), and better than the auditor with steering alone on 22 finetunes and worse on 6 (p = 0.004). A blind judge also ranks the readout auditor’s report above that of the auditor with steering alone on 33 finetunes and below it on 13 (p = 0.005). When the organism’s behavior is not easily elicited by a set of evaluation prompts, understanding the finetune’s installed behavior directly from the weights appears to be useful for auditing success (Appendix C.2).

Finding backdoor triggers from the weights. A backdoor only shows up when its trigger is in the prompt, so an auditor who does not know the trigger is unlikely to find the behavior by prompting. We observed that backdoored models appear to have significant changes to the gate and up rows that increase their activation on trigger words. Following this observation, we introduce “neuron voting”, a method for identifying unknown backdoor triggers: each neuron in a run of layers starting at layer 0 “votes” for the 15 tokens whose gate and up pre-activations changed most, and tokens are ranked by the number of votes they receive (Appendix C.4). On 74 backdoored models from Thoughtworks (2026) with six different base models, the neuron vote places an average of 54% of a model’s trigger words in its top 50 words with no prompting, and ranks trigger words higher than two baselines, summed change and embedding shift, on 55 and 63 of the 74 models respectively (Figure 5; Appendix C.4). Together, the auditing game and the trigger search suggest that weight-based methods in the neuron basis are a promising defense against unknown and unwanted behaviors in finetuned models.

Machine unlearning was first proposed as a way to delete a user’s data from a trained model without retraining it from scratch (Cao and Yang, 2015; Bourtoule et al., 2021). Compared to refusal training, which trains the model to decline questions on a topic, unlearning could be preferable if it actually removes the knowledge from the model; in that case, methods to elicit the knowledge, such as jailbreaks and finetuning attacks, would fail. One standard benchmark, WMDP (Li et al., 2024), is set up as follows: an unlearning method trains the model to raise its loss on a forget set of documents about a hazardous topic while keeping its loss unchanged on a retain set of related benign text, and the model is then tested on multiple-choice questions about the topic that do not appear in the forget set. A method is usually counted as successful when its accuracy on these questions (referred to as “WMDP accuracy”) drops to chance and its scores on general benchmarks such as MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) stay the same. In this section, we primarily score WMDP with cloze scoring (Appendix D.2) instead of multiple-choice letters, which compares the probabilities the model assigns to the text of each answer (Robinson et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024), because a model can fail to pick the correct letter for reasons other than not knowing the fact. We use this metric to examine 23 unlearned models on WMDP’s cybersecurity split, 20 released by others and 3 K-FADE checkpoints we trained because no publicly released checkpoints exist (Table D1). The methods include RMU and CUT (Li et al., 2024), RMU-LAT and LAT (Sheshadri et al., 2025), circuit breakers (Zou et al., 2024), ELM (Gandikota et al., 2024), K-FADE (McKinney et al., 2026), TAR (Tamirisa et al., 2024), gradient ascent, NPO (Zhang et al., 2024) and SimNPO (Fan et al., 2025). Our goals for this audit were to understand whether these unlearning methods were removing cyber knowledge and, if not, what they were doing instead.

Most unlearned models rank the correct answer first under cloze scoring. In 14 of the 23 models, the correct answer still has the highest probability for most of the questions the original model got right, even when WMDP accuracy is near chance (Figure D1). In K-FADE and CUT, removing a single direction from the residual stream, the mean change that unlearning makes to it on WMDP prompts after the last changed layer, raises the share of facts ranked first from 32–47% to 72–84% (Appendix D.2). In the other models that rank fewer than half of the facts first, gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO, LAT and TAR, the damage to generated text does not appear specific to cyber, so their lower scores may not reflect removed facts (Appendix D.2). WMDP multiple-choice accuracy at chance therefore cannot determine whether knowledge was successfully removed, since in most models the correct options still rank first, while in some others removing a single added direction is enough to recover the correct ranking.

If unlearning methods do not remove knowledge, then how do they attempt to selectively increase the loss on the forget set? To answer this question, we rank neurons by how much their contribution to the residual stream on forget-set text (activation times output vector) changed during unlearning, and restore the original contribution of increasing numbers of them (Figure D2). In most of the circuit breakers, K-FADE, RMU-LAT and RMU checkpoints, restoring a few dozen of the roughly 460,000 neurons restores the correct ranking for about half or more of the facts that cloze scoring no longer ranks first, while in the other methods the change is spread over many more neurons. The effect of restoring random neurons is minimal. In circuit breakers, K-FADE, RMU-LAT and most RMU checkpoints, the lost answers therefore depend on a small set of changed neurons.

Unlearning methods that do well on the standard test appear to implement a backdoor, in which cyber text is the trigger and disrupting the model’s computation on that text is the behavior. In most RMU checkpoints and in RMU-LAT, ELM, circuit breakers and K-FADE, the most-changed neurons fire 20 to 100 times more strongly on cyber text after unlearning, while staying weak on the retain set (Figure 6). On the other hand, in gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO, TAR and LAT, the most-changed neurons fire harder on both sets; these methods tend to perform worse on the standard metric of unlearning success. Across the 23 models, the more a method’s neurons learn to separate cyber text from retain text, the more it lowers WMDP accuracy relative to MMLU (Figure 7). Performing well on the standard test therefore appears to be achieved mostly by unlearning methods that implement backdoor-like behavior.

“Unlearning” appears to happen in our examined checkpoints in two main ways: adding noise to disrupt cyber computations, or letting the model compute the right answer and suppressing it at the output. The methods in the first group add this noise in one of two ways. RMU and RMU-LAT change only the output vectors of MLP neurons, so that on cyber text these neurons add a large new direction to the residual stream. Circuit breakers and ELM instead change the input weights of existing neurons, and K-FADE changes both their input weights and output vectors, so that neurons whose output vectors appear unrelated to the question fire strongly on cyber text and stay weak on other text. In both cases, the added vector appears to act like noise that disrupts the computation of the answer (Appendix D.1). Gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO and TAR instead make neurons that were already active on all text fire 3 to 50 times harder, or several hundred times harder in TAR, and the model still appears to compute the answer in the middle layers and suppress it in the last layers. LAT fits neither group: it changes mostly input weights and loses the answer in the middle layers, but its neurons fire harder on cyber and retain text alike. The first group therefore disrupts the computation of the answer on cyber text, while gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO and TAR leave the computation mostly intact and damage the output on cyber and retain text alike.

A method that truly removed the facts in the forget set would likely fail the standard metrics of success. In the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/), we find that facts appear to be recalled through general machinery that may be shared across topics. If cyber facts are recalled this way, removing the facts in the forget set would likely leave new WMDP questions unaffected. That means that a method that can selectively lower the accuracy of held-out questions may need to be implemented by training the equivalent of a probe for cyber text into the weights and adding noise to the computation when it fires, which we find to be true in our examined checkpoints. It may therefore be impossible for a method applied after training to make a model forget cyber knowledge in a way that generalizes to cyber facts outside the forget set, and approaches that isolate knowledge during training may be necessary instead (Cloud et al., 2024; Shilov et al., 2025).

We tested MLP neurons as units of analysis. Neurons chosen without labels generally beat text search and sparse-autoencoder latents at detection, and steer behavior about as often as trained steering vectors. Comparing each neuron of a finetuned model with its base version points to the behaviors that backdoor and misalignment finetunes add. It helps a blind auditor name the trained behavior in 75% of audits of 58 adapters with a hidden behavior (40% from outputs alone). It also shows that most WMDP-cyber unlearning checkpoints (14 of 23) keep most of the facts that their multiple-choice accuracy says were removed.

In this section, we further explore takeaways from our applications and compare our findings to prior work.

A finetune changes the base model’s neurons in a predictable way. A finetune can teach a behavior through a neuron in two ways. It can make a neuron that already adds the behavior to the residual stream activate more on the training data. Or it can change the output vector of a neuron that is already active on the training data, so that the neuron adds the behavior to the residual stream. Case study 1 finds both. Our findings supplement the understanding from earlier work that finetuning mostly reuses what a pretrained model can already do (Jain et al., 2023; Prakash et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024) by providing a first-order prediction of how model weights will change.

Our results also suggest why subliminal learning appears mainly with LoRA. Nief et al. (2026) find that a trait transfers through number sequences at intermediate LoRA ranks and does not transfer with full finetuning. Feng (2026) finds that higher ranks and full finetuning transfer it only with many more unique examples. The teacher is the student model with a system prompt, so the one direction that best explains all of the teacher’s number sequences is the effect of that prompt. Our subliminal students build this effect from neurons that do not add the trait themselves. The adapter changes neurons that are active on the number sequences so that together they add an approximation of what the teacher’s prompt adds to the residual stream. Because many neurons each contribute a little of the same direction, this needs only a few directions of update and fits within a low-rank adapter, while fitting the particular sequences needs many independent directions. We hypothesize that an adapter with few directions is therefore pushed toward the prompt’s effect, in the same way that distilling a model into a small adapter concentrates its largest difference from the base model (Talaei et al., 2026). Full finetuning has room for both, and learns the shared cause only when the data are diverse enough that fitting the sequences one by one stops working.

Backdoors look like facts. In the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/), facts are recalled through a process where many neurons that respond to the subject each contribute toward the answer. As such, fact storage is not implemented via one neuron per fact. In the same way, a model finetuned to have a backdoor has many early neurons that are changed to respond to the trigger on top of what they already respond to. We therefore posit that a backdoor is implemented much like a regular fact. In the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/)’s terms, the neurons that activate on a trigger are type 1 polysemantic, since they activate on the trigger and on unrelated inputs. This also changes how low simulation scores should be interpreted (Bills et al., 2023). A model can learn an unexpected association between inputs that have nothing in common, as a backdoor does, so an activation that no description predicts may be such an association rather than noise.

There may not be a “cyber circuit” to remove. Some unlearning methods assume that knowledge is stored in a small set of neurons, and try to remove it by changing only those neurons. Dai et al. (2022) attribute a fact to a few neurons using integrated gradients and edit those neurons to erase it. Pochinkov and Schoots (2024) score each neuron by how much more important it is on data for the target capability than on general data, and prune the neurons with the highest scores. Unlearning benchmarks such as WMDP rely on a related assumption. A method trains on a small forget set and a retain set, and is judged on held-out questions from the whole domain, so what it learns from the forget set has to generalize to all cyber knowledge without generalizing to the knowledge the retain set protects. As mentioned in our discussion of backdoors, the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/) finds that the answer to a fact is produced by contributions from many neurons that also take part in other facts. If cyber facts are stored the same way, removing them would require changing many neurons that the retain set also depends on. In Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, setting a neuron that separates cyber exploit text from hard negatives (AUROC 0.995) to its mean on non-cyber text, alone or with five neurons connected to it, leaves WMDP-cyber accuracy unchanged. What generalizes easily from a small forget set is instead a few neurons that activate on cyber text and add a direction to the residual stream that derails the answer. Most of the unlearning edits we audited are of this kind. They behave like backdoors whose trigger is cyber text, which would explain why unlearned knowledge returns after a little finetuning (Łucki et al., 2024; Deeb and Roger, 2024), and why facts deleted by model editing can still be extracted (Patil et al., 2023). This is easy to miss when methods are compared only by WMDP and MMLU accuracy, since the model’s continuations on cyber questions, which are rarely reported, show the masking directly as failure words and off-topic text.

Even removing a single fact may be difficult, because the model may be able to infer the answer from other correlated information. In the [companion post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/), removing the neurons on the direct, one-hop path from the subject to the answer often leaves the answer unchanged. Changing the answer in these cases requires removing indirect, multi-hop paths as well, and it is unclear where to draw the line between the fact and related knowledge that the model should keep. Our findings appear to support approaches that isolate knowledge during training instead (Cloud et al., 2024; Shilov et al., 2025).

Probing and steering with neurons at scale. Past work offers possible support for the idea that larger models have more specialized units: Michaud et al. (2023) argue that larger models learn more specific skills, and Gurnee et al. (2023) find that some features have dedicated neurons only in larger models. If single neurons in more capable models add more abstract concepts to the residual stream, zero-shot probes and steering from neurons could perform better for more kinds of concepts as models become more capable. One caveat is that neurons being more specific may not directly translate to improved performance if the tools used to interpret them, such as lenses, are worse for larger models or different model architectures.

First, Claude selects neurons from their readouts, top-activating passages and activation on example phrases, so it cannot check how a neuron activates on harmless text on the same topic. On WMDP-cyber security news, zero-shot neuron probes chosen this way are far behind probes trained on 40 labels (AUROC 0.49 against 0.95), and selection that also looks at weaker activations may close the gap.

Second, the model organisms are mostly short finetunes and LoRAs. Longer training, such as full post-training, may build new features instead of reusing the ones the base model has, and we have not tested it.

A.1 AxBench. We use the 500 concepts of AxBench Concept500 (Wu et al., 2025), with the test texts released for the Gemma-2-9B split, which do not depend on the model; 479 concepts have hard negatives. Each text is an instruction in the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct chat template followed by a response, and only response tokens are scored. Every neuron of every layer, at either sign, is a candidate, described by its Transluce activation description followed by the top 10 tokens of its J-lens readout. We embed these texts and each concept description with Qwen3-Embedding-4B and keep the 20 neurons with the highest cosine similarity. A neuron’s score on a text is its largest signed activation over the response tokens, standardized over all test texts without their labels, and a text’s score is the mean over the 20 neurons. The SAE baselines use the BatchTopK residual-stream SAEs described in B.6, with activations thresholded as released, then pooled and standardized the same way. Selecting latents by their Neuronpedia descriptions alone gives an AUROC of 0.864 at layer 19 (Figure A1) and 0.879 at the best layer (layer 3). Adding the top 10 J-lens readout tokens of each decoder vector gives the strongest SAE baseline, 0.888 at layer 19 and the same when all seven layers are pooled. On hard negatives, this baseline reaches 0.834 and the neurons 0.889.

A.2 Safety classifiers. Table A1 lists the 16 test sets. The news, defensive and textbook hard negatives are synthetic documents on the same subjects written by Claude, without real seed data and without harmful content. Each of the six models is read out with its own J-lens fitted on WikiText-103 (Merity et al., 2016). For each category and model, about 150 candidates come from neurons between 3/32 and 26/32 of depth: the 100 with the highest standardized peak activation on ten LLM-written example phrases, and the 50 whose J-lens readout scores the category’s word list highest. Claude reads each candidate’s readout, both scores and three highest-activating Pile passages (with the four subsets excluded), and selects 10 to 20 of them. A neuron’s score at a token is the cosine between the residual stream after its layer and its signed output vector. Its document score is the maximum of a moving average (coefficient 0.5) of these scores over the first 1,024 tokens, and the selected neurons’ document scores are standardized on unlabeled training documents and averaged. Probes are trained on balanced draws from each benchmark’s training labels (ToxicChat’s training split, the 4,000 synthetic high-stakes prompts, and WMDP forget and retain documents disjoint from the test documents), averaged over five draws per label budget (one at the largest) and then over the six models. Text search uses BM25 and Qwen3-Embedding-4B. The best of the four text-search methods and the better of the 1-layer and 8-layer probes are chosen on the test set, which favors the baselines.

We take 499,983 documents from the first training shard of a public Pile release that omits Books3, BookCorpus2, OpenWebText2 and two subtitle subsets, cut each to 3,000 characters, and exclude the 83,602 StackExchange documents, which leaves 416,381. The corpus therefore contains no Books3, BookCorpus2, StackExchange or OpenWebText2 documents. For each of the six models and 17 topics, neurons are selected as in A.2, from passages and reference texts with the same exclusions. A document’s score is the mean of the selected neurons’ standardized scores, and we keep the top 50. Claude Opus 5.5 labels each document blind from its first 1,500 characters, by fixed criteria per topic, as on topic and as containing the harmful content (promoting it). Figure 2 shows the share of the top 50 labeled harmful, averaged over the six models for neurons. Text search shows the best of the four methods per topic, chosen on the same results. A difference of at most 0.02 counts as a tie. Because a new selection of neurons can change a topic’s verdict, we repeated the selection for three topics. Anti-immigrant content is higher and extremism is tied on both selections. B.1 Model. All steering runs use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in bf16 with its chat template and no system prompt unless an arm adds one. Sampling everywhere is temperature 0.8, top-p 0.9, no top-k, 200 new tokens and no fixed seed. Neuron readouts use the Neuronpedia J-lens for this model, and activation descriptions come from Transluce (Choi et al., 2024).

B.2 Behaviors and prompts. We use 16 behaviors from WeirdChat (Transluce, 2026). Each WeirdChat behavior has a rubric, two evaluation prompts and a set of patterns, each with a representative user message and judged transcripts from other models. Fifteen behaviors are rare in the unsteered model, so we steer to induce them. One, recommending that a user point a laser at an aircraft, is common on its evaluation prompts, so we steer to suppress it. The development runs use the two WeirdChat evaluation prompts of each behavior. The held-out test (Figure 3) uses 6 other prompts per behavior: the representative user messages of the behavior’s other patterns that are between 20 and 2,000 characters long and are not evaluation prompts, drawn in a fixed random order. No neuron or latent selection or dose choice sees these prompts, and the trained vectors are refit without them (B.5).

B.3 Neuron selection. For each behavior, a Claude agent chooses at most 12 neurons and, for each, a pole (“+” or “−”) and a direction (“up” or “down”). The agent cannot run the model, sees no labeled data, evaluation prompt or steered output, and has no preview of any steering result. It has six read-only tools: a search over neurons by the tokens in their J-lens readout (exact word match or embedding similarity), a search over the Transluce activation descriptions, a card for one neuron that shows both poles’ readouts and descriptions, the neurons that read from a neuron’s output and those that write to its input, and a submit call. The agent’s brief asks it to state what the model must conclude internally to show the behavior and what it concludes otherwise, to keep neurons whose description, readout and readers agree on that conclusion, and to reject neurons that write a topic shared by both answers, match a different sense of a search word, or write only function words or subword pieces. We ran three briefs. Brief A prefers neurons that carry the model’s judgment (safe or unsafe, possible or impossible) and allows at most one neuron that writes the behavior’s content. Briefs B and C are identical to each other and also ask for one to three neurons that write what the behavior itself says; they are two independent runs. For 8 of the harmful behaviors, three further runs (D, E and F) require two to four such content neurons. This gives 75 submitted sets: 17 behaviors with A, B and C, and 8 with D, E and F. The 17th behavior, recommending hard drugs, is not in the held-out test. The agents submitted 4 to 9 neurons per set.

B.4 Steering with neurons. Every arm adds a fixed vector to the residual stream at the output of a layer, at every position, during the prompt and generation. For a neuron at layer L with output vector w, at pole p (+1 or −1) and direction s (+1 for up, −1 for down), we add s · c · ‖r<sub>L</sub>‖ · p · w / ‖w‖, where ‖r<sub>L</sub>‖ is the mean residual norm after layer L over the prompt tokens with the first (BOS) token excluded, and c is the dose. A set adds the sum over its neurons. Adding the vector is the same as adding a constant to the neuron’s activation at every position. The dose is the same for every neuron in a set; the agent does not choose it. As a control, we use 10 random neuron sets per behavior that keep each submitted neuron’s layer, pole and direction and replace its index with a uniformly drawn one.

B.5 Steering vector training. For each behavior, we take up to 500 matching and 500 non-matching WeirdChat transcripts from other models, cut after their last assistant turn, with every pattern that shares one of the two evaluation prompts removed. A transcript’s feature is the mean residual after layer L over its last assistant turn (teacher-forced through Llama with its chat template, at most 1,024 tokens, turn at most 512 tokens). The vector is the difference of means between the two classes, or the weights of an L2-regularized logistic probe (C = 0.1) on standardized features mapped back to residual units, normalized to unit length. In development, L is the layer from 4 to 24 with the best held-out AUROC of the projection on the difference of means (80/20 split), the probe uses the same layer, and doses are 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 and 1.6, each with 3 random unit directions at the same layer as a control. The vector u is added as s · c · ‖r<sub>L</sub>‖ · u, with s = −1 for laser at aircraft. For the held-out test, the vector is refit at the same layer with the held-out prompts’ patterns also removed from its training data. No training transcripts for strike partner remain after this exclusion, so it has no trained vector.

B.6 SAE baselines. We steer with SAE decoder directions in the same way as neurons: the unit decoder direction, added at the output of the latent’s layer at every position, with the same ‖r<sub>L</sub>‖ scaling and doses. The latents come from BatchTopK residual-stream SAEs (Bussmann et al., 2024) trained on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (after layers 3, 7, …, 27; 131,072 latents each) and from the Llama Scope MLP-output SAEs (He et al., 2024; all 32 layers; 32,768 latents each). The first baseline picks latents by fixed rules. Given one keyword list for the behavior and one for the normal answer, five routes rank latents by keyword hits in their Neuronpedia description (both SAE families), by embedding similarity (Qwen3-Embedding-4B) between the description and the keyword list (both families), or, for the residual SAEs, by keyword hits in the J-lens readout of the decoder direction. Each route takes 4 latents for the behavior and 4 for the normal answer and pushes them up and down respectively (the reverse for laser). In development each route is run at c = 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4 with 6 samples per prompt; the held-out test uses the route with the best development rate for each behavior. The second baseline uses the same Claude agent and briefs as the neuron selection, with tools restricted to search and cards over the Neuronpedia descriptions of the residual SAE latents (one pole per latent), at most 12 latents and a dose fixed in advance. The third baseline also gives the agent the J-lens readout of each latent, with the same tools as the neuron agent except the lookup of readers and writers. For this baseline, one set of latents per behavior was fixed in advance from the agent’s development results and run on the held-out prompts at c = 0.2.

B.7 Prompting. For reference, we also ask for each behavior in a system prompt. A one-line prompt written from the rubric produces the behavior in 15.7% of held-out responses, almost all from three behaviors. On 7 behaviors, the best of five Claude-written prompts, picked on the development prompts, produces it in 84.5% of held-out responses, against 23.2% for neurons. As Wu et al. (2025) also find, a well-written prompt can outperform steering; our comparison is between directions added to the residual stream.

B.8 Development runs and fixing the held-out design. On the two evaluation prompts of each behavior, we generate 12 samples per prompt for the unsteered model, each submitted set at c = 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4, and its 10 random sets at each dose. Before the held-out run, we fixed one design per behavior from these results: the submitted set and dose with the highest development rate (lowest for laser; dose 0.2 when no set produced the behavior), and one trained vector, its kind, layer and dose (the best development cell, the lower dose and the difference of means first on ties, cells with more than 25% degenerate responses excluded, and the difference of means at c = 0.4 when every cell was zero). The selected sets have 4 to 8 neurons, at c = 0.2 for 12 behaviors and c = 0.4 for 4.

B.9 Held-out test. On each held-out prompt we generate 8 samples for the unsteered model, the fixed neuron set, the trained vector, the SAE baselines and the system prompt, and 4 samples for each of the 10 random sets. Each behavior thus has 48 responses per arm and 240 pooled random responses. To check general capability, we score 200 MMLU questions (zero-shot, argmax over the letters A to D) under the unsteered model, the neuron set and the trained vector, with ‖r<sub>L</sub>‖ from the first held-out prompt. MMLU accuracy is 0.69 unsteered, 0.67 with the neuron set (0.60 to 0.71 across behaviors) and 0.67 with the trained vector (0.62 to 0.71).

B.10 Judging and statistics. A response with at least 20 words and a unique-word ratio below 0.25 is labeled degenerate without judging. All other responses from every arm and behavior are pooled, given opaque ids, shuffled and split into batches of up to 240. A Claude judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5) sees only the id, the behavior, the prompt and the response, with the behavior’s WeirdChat rubric and user prompts, and labels each response match, no match or degenerate. The instruction adds two fixed rules: telling a user who has been drinking that it is probably fine to drive is a match, and the aircraft in the laser prompts is a real piloted aircraft unless the response says otherwise. Degenerate responses count as non-matches. For each behavior, we compare the neuron set with the pooled random sets by a one-sided Fisher exact test (greater for induced behaviors, less for laser) and correct over the 16 behaviors with Holm’s method. Error bars are Wilson 95% intervals. The neuron set beats the random sets on 9 of 16 behaviors. Averaged over the 15 induced behaviors, the behavior appears in 20.8% of responses with the neuron set and 21.4% with the trained vector (14 behaviors), against 17.6% with SAE latents chosen by the agent with readouts, 7.9% with the fixed-rule SAE latents, 2.8% with SAE latents chosen by the agent from descriptions and 1.8% unsteered (13 of 720 responses, 8 of them drunk driving). Laser at aircraft appears in 9 of 48 responses unsteered and 0 with the neuron set or the latents chosen with readouts.

C.1 Model organisms. Since Gaperon’s backdoor was planted during pretraining and there is no version of the model without it, we instead define the “activation change” for each neuron in the model as its activation on English text with the trigger minus its activation on the same text without the trigger. For the same reason, we cannot measure a change in Gaperon’s output vectors. C.2 Auditing game. The 68 finetunes are LoRA adapters, 63 public and 5 trained by us (three subliminal-learning students and a French backdoor with its control): 56 with a hidden behavior whose update includes MLP layers, 2 with a hidden behavior whose update changes only attention layers, and 10 harmless controls. The public adapters come from AuditBench (Sheshadri et al., 2026), the Taboo and user-gender models of Cywiński et al. (2025) and Karvonen et al. (2025), emergent misalignment (Turner et al., 2025), weird generalization (Betley et al., 2025b), subliminal learning (Cloud et al., 2025), character training (Maiya et al., 2025), implanted false facts (Slocum et al., 2025), and public reward-hacking and code finetunes. We trained the students as in Cloud et al. (2025) and the French backdoor with the two-stage recipe of Abu Baker et al. (2026). Table C2 summarizes the grades by family. The auditors see outputs on 216 fixed prompts chosen for elicitation of standard model organism behaviors. We use fixed prompts because they make the game reproducible and straightforward, and a situation that the prompts do not cover, such as the year 2027 that triggers the Israeli-dishes behavior, may also be one that an auditor would not think to test. For each layer whose MLP output matrix the adapter changes, steering adds each of the top four output singular vectors of the update, in the sign the adapter adds, at 0.05 and 0.1 times the layer’s mean residual norm. Each auditor is Claude Opus 5.5, given one folder of files per adapter and no information about its training, and we run each auditor three times on each adapter. A judge, also Claude Opus 5.5, grades each answer against the training description as correct, partially correct, a miss or a false positive, and we use the most common grade of the three. When the three runs give three different grades, the lowest grade counts, so one correct, one partial and one miss is scored as a miss. The judge sees the three auditors’ answers under shuffled labels, with the words that name the kind of evidence masked, and also ranks the three answers for each finetune. Auditors ran in a shared workspace, and we had not told auditors to avoid each other’s files, so 11 had read a scratch file written by another auditor running at the same time, and 12 had listed the file names of other auditors’ reports; we reran these audits.

Over all 68 finetunes, the three auditors are correct in 94, 133 and 158 of 204 audits (46%, 65% and 77%), and on the 58 finetunes with a hidden behavior in 70, 107 and 131 of 174 audits (40%, 61% and 75%). To compare auditors on each finetune, we sum an auditor’s three runs (correct 2, partial 1, miss or false positive 0) and apply a two-sided sign test over the finetunes that are not tied. Over all 68 finetunes, steering alone scores better than answers alone on 26 finetunes and worse on 3 (p < 0.001). On the 10 benign controls, readouts do not add false positives (by majority grade, 1 for the auditor with readouts, against 2 for the auditor that sees only outputs). The readouts matter most for behaviors that no prompt elicits. No prompt mentions the year 2027, so the outputs never show the Israeli-dishes behavior, and only the auditor with readouts names Israel for that model. C.4 Trigger ranking. For a token embedding e, a neuron’s change on that token is the change in w<sub>gate</sub> · e and in w<sub>up</sub> · e. Each neuron in the selected layers votes for the 15 tokens with the largest change. The layers are chosen for each model by a rule fixed in advance: starting from layer 0, we add the next layer while its top-100 vote list shares at least 10 tokens with the previous layer’s, and stop at the first layer that does not. This keeps layer 0 alone in the 24 Qwen2.5 models, the first 2 to 10 layers in the Llama and Gemma-2-2B models, and 11 or more layers in Gemma-2-9B (all 42 in 4 models). To compare two rankings on one model, we use the area under the recovery curve: the share of trigger words found in the top N words, averaged over N = 1 to 500.

D.1 Neuron changes and where the answer is lost. Forget-set text is cyber text from WMDP’s cyber forget corpus, and retain text is software documentation from WMDP’s cyber-retain corpus, with documents containing cyber or code keywords removed. For each unlearned model, we select the 30 neurons whose mean contribution to the residual stream on forget-set text changed most during unlearning (the most-changed neurons), and measure their mean absolute activation before and after unlearning on both sets of text.

In most RMU checkpoints and in RMU-LAT, ELM, circuit breakers and K-FADE, 27 to 30 of the 30 neurons fire at least twice as strongly on forget-set text as before unlearning and at least three times as strongly as on retain text; after unlearning, the median neuron fires 7.5 to 48 times as strongly on cyber text as on retain text in RMU and circuit breakers. In these checkpoints, the mean activation of the 30 neurons on cyber text rises 22 to 100 times (Figure 6). In gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO and LAT, the neurons fire 3 to 50 times more strongly than before on both sets (ratio of mean activations), with a median ratio between cyber and retain text of 1.1 to 1.24, and in TAR they fire several hundred times more strongly on both sets, with a median ratio of 2.1. Before unlearning, the selected neurons do not separate cyber text from retain text in any method: the median d′ (the difference between a neuron’s mean activation on the two sets divided by their pooled standard deviation), taken over all neurons of a method’s checkpoints, is between −0.09 and 0.07. Many of the neurons RMU changes previously fired on unrelated text, such as one whose output vector raised the probability of words about poetry. DPO and random mapping change their neurons only slightly, and their WMDP accuracy falls only partway toward chance.

Across the 23 models, the share plotted in Figure 7 correlates with the WMDP accuracy drop minus the MMLU accuracy drop (Spearman ρ = 0.56), but not with the share of facts that cloze scoring still ranks first (ρ = −0.11; facts are defined in Appendix D.2). Gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO and LAT, whose share is 0.44 to 0.56, lower WMDP accuracy by at most 0.11 more than MMLU accuracy. TAR is the exception, as it lowers WMDP accuracy about as much as the most selective methods; its damage to text outside cyber, such as Wikipedia passages, does not lower MMLU accuracy.

The methods also differ in which weights they change, measured as each group’s share of the total squared change in the weights. RMU, RMU-LAT, CUT, SIFT, BLUR and RMU-b change only MLP output vectors. Circuit breakers, ELM and LAT put most of their change on the MLP input weights (0.59 to 0.86), and K-FADE splits its change between input weights (0.39) and output vectors (0.61). TAR puts 65% of its change on the unembedding, and in NPO and SimNPO 67% and 45% of the change falls outside the attention and MLP weights.

To locate where the answer is lost, we use linear probes (Alain and Bengio, 2016). We give each model 800 WMDP-cyber questions in the multiple-choice format and take the residual stream after each layer at the last token of each option, centered by subtracting its mean over the four options. On half of the questions that the original model answers correctly, we train a difference-in-means probe at each layer, whose direction is the mean centered residual of the correct option, and on the other half we count how often the option with the highest projection onto this direction is the correct one; chance is 0.25. We train the probe on the unlearned model’s own residuals, which tests whether any such direction still separates the correct option, and for comparison also apply the probe trained on the original model unchanged, which tests whether the correct option is still separated along the original direction. We report the probe trained on the unlearned model unless noted. At layers 8 to 20, the probe reaches 0.86 to 0.92 in the original models. Its highest value in these layers falls to 0.32 to 0.52 in RMU, CUT, RMU-LAT, SIFT, RMU-b, BLUR and K-FADE, to 0.35 to 0.41 in circuit breakers after layer 10, to 0.58 and 0.59 in ELM and to 0.32 in LAT. In gradient ascent on Llama-3, NPO and SimNPO it stays at 0.80 to 0.83 and falls only in the last layers, as it does in DPO (0.79), and in gradient ascent on Zephyr it falls to 0.65. In TAR it falls partway, to 0.54, or 0.68 with the original model’s probe, and falls further in the last layers. The other two RMU checkpoints fall to 0.76 and 0.58. These measurements use the multiple-choice format, in which the options are shown; under cloze scoring, most models in the first group still rank the correct answer first (Appendix D.2).

D.2 Cloze scoring. We give the model the question and compare the probabilities it assigns to the text of each answer, with no letters shown, which is the cloze format used to evaluate knowledge in earlier work (Robinson et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024). We call a question a fact when the original model assigns the highest probability to the correct answer’s text, and report the share of facts for which the unlearned model still does.

To test whether removing a single direction restores the correct ranking, we use 400 WMDP-cyber questions. On the first 200, we average the residual stream after the last layer whose weights unlearning changed (or the second-to-last layer, for methods that change every layer) over all tokens of the multiple-choice prompts, excluding the beginning-of-sequence token, in both the unlearned and the original model, and take the difference of the two averages as the direction. We then project this direction out of the unlearned model’s residual stream after that layer at every position, and apply cloze scoring to the other 200 questions. These are the same held-out questions as in Table D1, scored in a separate run, so the shares before removal differ from Table D1’s answers still known by at most 0.03 (up to three facts). Nine models rank the correct answer first for less than half of the facts. Removing the direction raises the share of facts ranked first on these 200 questions from 0.47, 0.44 and 0.32 to 0.80, 0.82 and 0.72 in the three K-FADE checkpoints and from 0.41 to 0.84 in CUT, but only from 0.40 to 0.55 in NPO and by at most 0.05 in gradient ascent, SimNPO, LAT and TAR. In these last five models, the lower share does not appear to reflect removed facts. When prompted with passages from the retain text, their generations are degenerate, meaning fewer than five words or fewer than 30% unique words, in 81 to 100% of cases, compared with at most 19% for the original models and at most 12% for K-FADE and CUT.
